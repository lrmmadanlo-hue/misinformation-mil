MO-SS033 The Contemporary World · Group 12

Combating Misinformation in the Digital Space

A Media and Information Literacy approach in the Philippines

Two in three Filipinos worry about telling real from fake online. We propose a short, measurable program that teaches people to check before they share.

The problemFalse information spreads fast on social media.
Our solutionA free workshop that teaches people to verify before sharing.
Our goal80% of 20 learners improve in 8 weeks.

01 · The problem

The problem: false information travels faster than corrections

Globalization and social media let information cross borders and reach large audiences within minutes. That access is valuable, but it also lets misleading content circulate widely before anyone checks it.

Misinformation

False or inaccurate information shared whether or not the sharer intends to deceive.

Disinformation

False or misleading information deliberately spread to deceive.

Scope: Filipinos who rely on digital platforms for news and communication need practical ways to judge what they read, in familiar languages and settings.

The scale in the Philippines

67%of Filipino adults are concerned about what is real or fake online, a record high since 2020 and above the 58% global average.
66%use social media for news every week, and 85% use some online source.
38%trust most news most of the time, so people are unsure where to turn.

Reuters Institute Digital News Report 2025, Philippines (survey conducted January–February 2025, during the midterm campaign period). Figures as reported by Chua (2025) and VERA Files (2025); see Sources.

This is a globalization issue because the same platforms and content flow across borders and arrive in Filipino feeds at once. Heavy reliance on them means a gap in verification skills affects everyone, not only experts.

Why it matters

  • Poorly informed decisions about social, political, health, or educational issues.
  • Confusion about events and public information.
  • Reduced trust in reliable journalism and public institutions.
  • Greater conflict and polarization between groups.
  • Difficulty telling verified information from misleading claims.

02 · Root causes

Root causes: why misinformation spreads

Individual factors

Some users share without checking the source, and confirmation bias makes content that matches existing beliefs feel more convincing.

Misleading content

Sensational headlines, manipulated images, missing context, and fabricated claims.

Platform and network factors

Rapid sharing and recommendation systems can help misleading content reach wider audiences.

Information-literacy gaps

People may lack the time, resources, or skills to verify claims and separate credible sources from unreliable ones.

Our solution targets the fourth cause directly, because it is the one a school or community can change without waiting for platforms or regulators.

03 · Who is involved

Stakeholders and existing initiatives

Department of Education

Supports media and information literacy through curriculum and learning resources.

Teachers, schools, universities

Teach verification skills and organize learning activities.

VERA Files and other fact-checkers

Investigate claims and publish evidence-based corrections.

Presidential Communications Office

Supports public information initiatives and fact-checking collaborations, including a training partnership with VERA Files.

Social media platforms

Provide reporting tools, content warnings, moderation, and transparency measures.

Students and the public

Evaluate information and decide responsibly before sharing.

04 · Weighing options

Comparing possible solutions

SolutionMain benefitMain limitation
Holding platforms accountable (for example, the EU Digital Services Act)Encourages transparency and better management of platform risks.Hard to enforce, and must protect freedom of expression.
Media and Information Literacy (MIL)Builds verification skills people can use on any platform.Needs teaching, practice, and regular updates.
Platform-based interventionsWarning labels, source links, and sharing prompts may interrupt the spread.Depends on platform cooperation and on fair, accurate application.

Other viewpoints matter here. Regulation supporters argue that individual skills cannot match algorithmic scale, while critics warn that heavy rules can chill legitimate speech. Our recommendation therefore pairs education with the other two approaches rather than replacing them.

05 · Our solution

Our proposed solution: a practical MIL learning program

A short learning program for students and community members on recognizing misleading claims, verifying information, evaluating sources, and thinking critically before sharing.

Participants will learn to

1Check the original source of a claim.
2Compare with multiple credible, independent sources.
3Check the date and context.
4Spot misleading headlines and manipulated images.
5Pause and verify before sharing.

How the program runs

Each session uses real Philippine examples from VERA Files fact-checks, short practice rounds, and a group discussion in English or Filipino. Participants take the same style of source-verification assessment before and after, so improvement can be measured fairly.

Sample assessment items (draft)
  1. A viral post claims a typhoon warning is happening today. What two things do you check first?
  2. Pick the most reliable evidence: a screenshot, an anonymous page, or a published correction from an independent fact-checker. Explain why.
  3. You find an old photo reused with a new caption. What clues show it is out of context?

Scored with a simple 0–10 rubric covering source, evidence, and context.

Why we selected it

MIL builds on existing DepEd and UNESCO resources, can be piloted in one school or community, and teaches skills that transfer across platforms. It also supports the Philippine context through local examples and familiar languages.

06 · The plan

From proposal to implementation

  1. Weeks 1–2Research existing solutions and Philippine misinformation cases.
  2. Weeks 3–4Develop learning materials and prepare activities, using free DepEd and UNESCO resources.
  3. Weeks 5–6Coordinate with partners, run the workshop, and give the source-verification assessment.
  4. Weeks 7–8Compare before-and-after results and revise the materials.
  5. One month laterRun a follow-up assessment to check retention.

Potential partners

Schools or universities, teachers and students, community organizations.

Resources needed

Educational materials, online platforms, assessment tools, and low-cost printed copies.

07 · Success measures

How we will measure success

Within eight weeks, run an MIL pilot for at least 20 participants, with at least 80% improving their scores on a source-verification assessment. A follow-up assessment one month later checks whether the skills stick.

Specific

Improve participants’ ability to verify sources and evaluate online information.

Measurable

At least 20 participants; at least 80% improve their assessment scores.

Achievable

Free learning materials and a small school or community pilot.

Relevant

Directly addresses the ability to identify and verify misinformation.

Time-bound

Pilot in eight weeks, follow-up one month later.

08 · Risks

Risks, challenges, and contingency plans

Possible challengeContingency plan
Limited funding or materialsUse free DepEd and UNESCO resources and low-cost printed materials.
Low workshop participationCoordinate with school or community leaders and offer flexible schedules.
Outdated examplesReview and update materials with recent, verified examples.
Limited internet accessProvide printed materials and offline exercises.
Distrust of fact-checking sourcesExplain the verification process and compare evidence from independent sources.
Little improvement in scoresIdentify difficult concepts, revise activities, and add practice.
Learning is not retainedRun follow-up activities and assessments one month later.

The pilot should be reviewed before wider rollout. If results are limited, organizers should identify the causes, improve the materials, and test the revised program again.

09 · Impact

Expected impact and recommendations

Expected impact

  • Better recognition of misleading claims and sources.
  • More thoughtful, responsible sharing habits.
  • Greater awareness of independent fact-checking.
  • Reusable, revisable learning materials.
  • Cooperation among schools, universities, and community groups.

Recommendations

  1. Strengthen MIL activities with Philippine examples.
  2. Give regular practice in source verification and fact-checking.
  3. Update materials as misinformation techniques change.
  4. Measure learning outcomes before expanding.
  5. Combine education with fact-checking and responsible platform practices.

Long-term view

MIL is a starting point, not a cure. Lasting results depend on sustained education, cooperation among stakeholders, and continued evaluation, and the pilot’s lessons should feed each new cohort.
